## 1. Motivation & why-this-company (why Ostrya, why AI, why now, 5-year, what excites you about the problem, why leave current role)

**Q1. Why Ostrya? Of all the AI companies you could be talking to right now, what pulled you toward an early-stage AI dev-tooling company specifically?**
- *What they're really assessing:* Genuine, specific mission-alignment vs. generic "I love AI" enthusiasm.
- *Strong answer:* Honestly, it started with your take-home — the cross-review PR bot. I've built a lot of AI tooling on my own, but that problem hit a nerve because code review is where I feel the most friction daily: it's high-context, high-stakes, and it's exactly the kind of judgment work I want agents to actually get right, not just autocomplete. I run about 80 live sites and tools solo, and the thing that unlocks that scale for me is tooling that removes toil — so building dev tooling for other engineers is basically my day-to-day pain turned into a product. Early-stage matters to me because I'm used to owning something end-to-end, from the CI pipeline to the deploy; I don't want to be one layer in a ten-layer org. And the "cross-review" framing tells me you're thinking about correctness and trust in agent output, which is the unsolved part — that's where I want to spend my next few years.
- *Watch out:* Praising "the AI space" or funding/hype instead of the actual product and problem.

**Q2. Why AI, and why now? What made this the thing you want to go deep on rather than staying a generalist?**
- *What they're really assessing:* Conviction and timing awareness vs. bandwagon-chasing.
- *Strong answer:* I didn't come to AI from the hype side — I came from automation. I've spent years wiring up pipelines with GitHub Actions, git-as-a-database, keyless AI calls, and lately MCP and multi-agent orchestration, because I'm allergic to doing the same task twice. What changed recently is that models finally got good enough to handle ambiguous, context-heavy work, so the bottleneck moved from "can the model do it" to "can we build the harness, tools, and guardrails around it" — and that harness-building is exactly my strength. Now is the moment because the primitives (tool use, MCP, agent loops) are stabilizing but the products on top are wide open. I'd rather be building those products while the ground is still soft than arrive after the patterns are all set.
- *Watch out:* "AI is the future / everyone's doing it" — timing with no personal throughline.

**Q3. Walk me through why you're looking to leave your current role at TCS. Be honest.**
- *What they're really assessing:* Maturity and honesty about the move without badmouthing.
- *Strong answer:* TCS gave me real fundamentals — I work as an SAP consultant, and enterprise consulting taught me discipline, how to operate inside constraints, and how to ship reliably for people who depend on it. But the ceiling for me isn't skills, it's surface area: my personal projects are where I get to own the whole thing, pick the architecture, and ship in a day instead of a quarter. I've essentially been running a second, faster company on nights and weekends, and I want that ownership and pace to be my actual job, not my side channel. There's no drama here — it's that the work I'm most alive in is agent and AI tooling, and TCS isn't where that lives. I want to bring the same reliability I learned there to a place where I also get to move fast.
- *Watch out:* Complaining about bureaucracy, pay, or managers — signals a running-away narrative.

**Q4. Where do you see yourself in five years? What does a great outcome look like for you?**
- *What they're really assessing:* Whether their trajectory fits the candidate's ambition, and realism.
- *Strong answer:* Five years out, I want to have been early on a product that a lot of engineers genuinely rely on — the kind of tool they'd notice if it disappeared. Concretely, I'd like to have grown from building features to owning a domain end-to-end: the agent behavior, the eval harness, the reliability story, maybe mentoring the next people who join. I'm not chasing a title; I measure myself by whether the thing I built shipped and held up under real load — that's the same bar I hold my 80-site fleet to, just with a team and much higher stakes. If Ostrya's the place where the AI dev-tooling bet plays out, I'd love to be one of the people whose fingerprints are on the core of it. What I want to avoid is being far from the actual building.
- *Watch out:* Vague "grow and learn" or, worse, a plan that clearly ends in leaving to start your own thing.

**Q5. Tell me about a time you took full ownership of something end-to-end — from an idea to something real people use — without anyone assigning it to you.**
- *What they're really assessing:* High agency and self-direction under zero supervision (STAR).
- *Strong answer:* Situation: I kept wanting a live dashboard for market mood — an hourly fear/greed tracker — and nothing free did it the way I wanted. Task: build and run it end-to-end, at zero cost, with no one asking me to. Action: I found a keyless public endpoint, wrote the fetch-and-store pipeline on GitHub Actions with git itself as the database so I paid nothing for storage, built an Astro gauge front-end, and put it live on a subdomain — then folded it into the same fleet conventions I use across all my sites so it maintains itself. Result: it's been running hands-off on an hourly cron ever since, one of ~80 things I keep live solo. The point isn't the tracker — it's that I'll take a vague itch all the way to a running, self-maintaining product without a ticket telling me to.
- *Watch out:* A "we" story where the candidate's specific decisions and ownership are invisible.

**Q6. What genuinely excites you about the code-review / agent-correctness problem specifically — not AI in general?**
- *What they're really assessing:* Depth of thought about the actual problem domain.
- *Strong answer:* What grabs me is that code review is a judgment problem disguised as a text problem. A naive bot flags style and misses the one race condition that matters; the hard part is context — knowing what this change is trying to do and where it can go wrong. When I did your take-home, the interesting tension was exactly that: how do you make the agent's review trustworthy enough that engineers don't start ignoring it, because a review tool that cries wolf is worse than none. That's a correctness-and-trust problem, which is where MCP and good tool design actually earn their keep — giving the agent the right context instead of hoping the prompt carries it. Getting that loop right, where the agent is genuinely additive to a senior reviewer, is the kind of problem I'd happily obsess over.
- *Watch out:* Generic "AI will change coding" futurism with no grasp of the review problem's real difficulty.

**Q7. Startups are messy — scope shifts, things break at 2am, priorities flip. Tell me about a time you dealt with that kind of ambiguity, and how you'd bring it here.**
- *What they're really assessing:* Startup grit and comfort with instability (STAR).
- *Strong answer:* Situation: keyless AI providers I depend on across the fleet change or break without warning — a model endpoint just stops responding. Task: keep dozens of live sites that call AI working without a paid key and without me babysitting each one. Action: I built provider failover into a shared client so calls fall through a ranked list of free providers, and I standardized the setup across every repo so a single fix propagates instead of me patching 80 places. Result: when a provider drops, the fleet degrades gracefully instead of going dark, and I fix it once. I'd bring the same instinct here: when something's on fire, stabilize first, then make the fix systemic so it can't bite twice — and I'm genuinely fine operating without a fully-drawn map, because that's how I already work.
- *Watch out:* Claiming to "love chaos" with no example, or a story where they just waited for direction.

**Q8. You clearly do a huge amount solo. How do you feel about giving up some of that control to work inside a team and follow decisions you didn't make?**
- *What they're really assessing:* Low ego and collaborative maturity — the flip side of high agency.
- *Strong answer:* It's a fair question, because I do run my own stuff my way. But my day job is the opposite — as an SAP consultant I ship inside a big org with plenty of decisions made above me, and I've learned to disagree well and then commit fully once a call is made. Honestly, the solo fleet taught me the value of a team more than anything: I'm the single point of failure for 80 things, and there's a real limit to what one person can carry or catch. I'd trade some autonomy for good code review and people who catch what I miss in a heartbeat — that's literally the product you're building. Strong opinions, held loosely, and I'd rather be proven wrong fast than right slowly.
- *Watch out:* Signaling they only thrive as a solo operator or subtly implying teammates slow them down.

**Q9. If you joined and six months in the company pivoted away from the exact thing that excited you today, how would you take that?**
- *What they're really assessing:* Mission commitment vs. attachment to one feature; resilience.
- *Strong answer:* I'd want to understand the why behind the pivot first — early-stage pivots usually mean you learned something real about what users actually need, and that's a feature of startups, not a bug. My attachment is to the mission of making AI genuinely useful and trustworthy for engineers, not to one specific bot; if the problem shifts but that mission holds, I'm still very much in. I've had to kill and rebuild plenty of my own projects when the original premise didn't hold up — I once verified that a whole assumption I was building on was just wrong, so I dropped it rather than defend it. What would actually bother me is a pivot with no learning behind it. But an honest "here's what we found, here's the new bet" — I'd roll up my sleeves.
- *Watch out:* Sounding like they'd disengage or leave if their pet feature dies.

**Q10. This is a question for you to ask me — what's the one thing you most want to understand about Ostrya before you'd say yes?**
- *Why it lands:* It shows the candidate evaluates opportunities like an owner and cares about the actual bet, not just the offer — CEOs respect being interviewed back on substance.
- *Follow-up:* "What has to be true 18 months from now for you to feel this bet on AI dev-tooling paid off — and where's the biggest risk you're still not sure how to de-risk?" A natural follow-up: "And when you picture the first few engineers who make this work, what do they have in common — what would make me a great fit versus just a capable one?"

## 2. Ownership, initiative, high-agency, self-direction, driving things end-to-end without being told

**Q1. Tell me about something you built and now run entirely on your own — from the first commit to keeping it alive in production. What did owning the whole thing actually look like?**
- *What they're really assessing:* Whether "end-to-end ownership" is a real muscle or a resume word.
- *Strong answer:* Over the last couple of years I've built and now run a fleet of roughly 80 live sites, tools, and APIs completely solo. The stack is deliberately boring and cheap — Astro on Cloudflare, GitHub Actions for scheduling and deploys, git itself as the database, and keyless AI providers with failover — so the whole thing runs at effectively $0. I own everything: I pick the architecture, write the code, wire the pipelines, and I'm also the one who gets paged when a cron job silently stops writing or a provider starts rate-limiting. The hardest part wasn't building any single site, it was designing so that 80 of them don't become 80 pagers — so I standardized a canonical config block and an AGENTS.md per repo so each one is self-explanatory and independently deployable. That constraint of "I am the only person who will ever fix this" forced better decisions than any team ever did.
- *Watch out:* Listing tech and traffic numbers but never mentioning failure, maintenance, or the unglamorous ops.

**Q2. Describe a time you saw a problem that wasn't assigned to you and no one asked you to fix — and you fixed it anyway.**
- *What they're really assessing:* Genuine initiative vs. only executing tickets handed down.
- *Strong answer:* When my fleet crossed a few dozen sites, I noticed I was quietly drowning in repeated ops toil — every new site meant hand-copying secrets, deploy config, and AI-provider fallback logic, and drift between them was starting to cause outages nobody had reported yet. Nobody asked me to fix it because I'm the only "nobody" there is, but the honest thing was that future-me was going to pay for it. So I stopped shipping features for a bit and built a single canonical config block plus a per-repo AGENTS.md that encodes the fleet rules inline, so each repo is standalone and enforceable. It wasn't glamorous and it delayed a launch, but it turned a growing class of silent failures into a solved problem. The lesson I took to heart is that initiative often looks like stopping to fix the thing that's about to bite you, not adding the next shiny feature.
- *Watch out:* Framing "initiative" as going rogue and ignoring priorities, rather than solving a real, justified problem.

**Q3. When you started our take-home — the cross-review PR bot — how did you decide what to build without a spec spelling everything out?**
- *What they're really assessing:* How they operate in ambiguity and self-direct toward the real goal.
- *Strong answer:* I treated the take-home the way I treat my own projects: figure out the actual job-to-be-done before writing code. The real goal of a cross-review PR bot isn't "call an LLM on a diff," it's to give a reviewer signal they can trust and act on quickly, so I anchored on precision and clear, reviewable output over cleverness. I made explicit decisions about scope — what a first version should confidently do versus punt on — and I left comments where I was making an assumption so you could correct my mental model rather than guess at it. Because I build AI agent and MCP tooling constantly on my own time, I leaned on patterns I already trust for orchestration and failover instead of inventing them under time pressure. Where I was unsure about your priorities, I optimized for the version I'd want to actually merge, and flagged the tradeoffs I'd revisit with more context.
- *Watch out:* "I just followed the instructions exactly" — signals they can't move without a fully-specified spec.

**Q4. Give an example of a project you drove where you had to make the call yourself, own the outcome, and there was no one to escalate to if it went wrong.**
- *What they're really assessing:* Comfort holding the bag — decision ownership under real consequences.
- *Strong answer:* Every one of my personal projects is exactly this, but a concrete one: choosing "git-as-database" across the fleet. There was no senior engineer to sanity-check it and no one else to blame if it fell over at 80 repos — it was purely my call and my consequence. I chose it deliberately because it gave me versioned history, zero hosting cost, and dead-simple recovery, and I accepted the tradeoff that it doesn't scale to high write concurrency, which my read-heavy workloads don't need. I set it up so that if a scheduled write failed, the failure was visible in git history rather than silently lost. It's held up across the whole fleet, but the point isn't that I was right — it's that I made the bet consciously, wrote down why, and owned that I'd be the one to migrate it if it ever stopped fitting.
- *Watch out:* Only citing decisions where a manager or teammate ultimately approved or absorbed the risk.

**Q5. High agency usually means doing more with less. Tell me about a time constraints forced you to be resourceful instead of asking for more resources.**
- *What they're really assessing:* Startup grit — resourcefulness over resource-requesting.
- *Strong answer:* My entire fleet is a running answer to this: I run ~80 live properties at $0 infrastructure cost with no team and no budget. The constraint was self-imposed but real — I refuse to let personal projects become a monthly bill — so instead of paying for hosting, a database, and AI API credits, I used Cloudflare's free tier, git as the store, and keyless AI providers with automatic failover so no single provider outage takes me down. The resourceful part wasn't any one trick, it was ranking free providers purely on capability and chaining them so quality stayed high without a credit card. Constraints like this actually make me design better, because "throw money at it" is off the table from day one. In a startup that instinct matters more than in a big shop, because runway is the real constraint.
- *Watch out:* Describing resourcefulness as heroics/burnout instead of smart design, or implying you'd just expense your way out.

**Q6. Tell me about a time you shipped something, watched it in the real world, and had to iterate because your first version was wrong.**
- *What they're really assessing:* Ownership past the ship date — accountability for outcomes, not output.
- *Strong answer:* Early on, my instinct with the fleet was to make each site clever and bespoke, and I shipped a batch that way. Watching them run, I realized the real failure mode wasn't any single bug — it was that every site was slightly different, so a fix in one didn't propagate and I kept re-solving the same problem. That was on me: I'd optimized for the demo, not for running 80 of these for years. So I iterated toward standardization — the shared config block and per-repo rules — even though it meant reworking things that already "worked." I tend to treat the ship date as the start of ownership, not the end, because in my world I'm the one who feels every regression directly. Being honest that my v1 architecture was wrong was cheaper than defending it.
- *Watch out:* Claiming everything shipped perfectly, or blaming users/requirements for the needed rework.

**Q7. You have a demanding day job as an SAP consultant at TCS and still build prolifically on the side. How do you actually drive personal projects to completion without anyone holding you accountable?**
- *What they're really assessing:* Self-direction and follow-through when there's zero external pressure.
- *Strong answer:* Honestly, the day job is part of why the personal work is disciplined — I have limited hours, so I can't afford to leave things half-built. I keep the accountability external-to-me even when no one's watching: everything is a live URL, so a broken project is publicly broken, which is a strong forcing function. I also design for completion by keeping scope tight and the stack boring, so "done and running" is reachable in the time I actually have rather than an endless rewrite. My CS fundamentals and the JEE-era habit of grinding hard problems help, but the real trick is shipping small, live, and often instead of waiting for a big perfect release. The through-line is that I'd rather have 80 real things running than one impressive thing that never launched.
- *Watch out:* Vague "I'm just passionate / disciplined" with no concrete mechanism, or implying the day job gets shortchanged.

**Q8. What draws you to building AI dev-tooling and agents — and why do it at an early-stage company like Ostrya rather than keep it as a side pursuit?**
- *What they're really assessing:* Mission alignment and whether the agency scales to the company's actual work.
- *Strong answer:* AI agents and dev-tooling are genuinely where I already spend my discretionary energy — multi-agent orchestration, MCP, automation pipelines are what I build for fun, so this isn't a pivot for me. What pulls me toward it is that these tools change what a single motivated person can ship, and I've felt that firsthand running a fleet no team my size should be able to run. The reason to do it inside an early-stage company rather than solo is leverage and stakes: alone I optimize for cost-zero personal tools, but I want to point the same end-to-end ownership at a product real users depend on, with people who are better than me to sharpen it. Ostrya's cross-review problem is exactly the kind of thing I'd tinker with anyway — I'd just rather build it seriously with a team than as another tab in my fleet. And early-stage suits how I work: I'm comfortable owning ambiguous things end-to-end without waiting to be told.
- *Watch out:* Generic "I love AI, it's the future" with no personal specificity or reason it must be *this* company.

**Q9. Tell me about a time you had to teach yourself something hard, fast, with no one to guide you — and shipped despite it.**
- *What they're really assessing:* Learning autonomy — the engine behind sustained high agency.
- *Strong answer:* MCP and multi-agent orchestration are a recent one — the space moves weekly, docs lag, and there's no senior person on my side of the fleet to ask. I learned it the way I learn everything now: build the smallest thing that exercises the concept, break it, read the actual protocol and source rather than blog summaries, and keep a working reference I can reuse. Concretely, I got keyless multi-provider AI with failover working across my sites by ranking providers on capability and wiring the fallback myself, which meant understanding each one's quirks the hard way. My CS fundamentals give me the floor to reason about the new stuff instead of cargo-culting it. The habit I trust most is that I'd rather learn by shipping a rough working version and refining than read for a week and build nothing.
- *Watch out:* Listing courses/certs consumed passively, with no evidence it turned into something shipped.

**Q10. (Question to ask the CEO) "A year in, if I'd been a clear success, what would I have owned end-to-end — and where would you have expected me to push back on you rather than just execute?"**
- *Why it lands:* It signals you assume real ownership and independent judgment, not task-taking, and it forces the CEO to describe what high agency concretely looks like inside their company — which tells you whether the culture actually rewards it.
- *Follow-up:* "And when someone here disagrees with your direction and turns out to be right, what usually has to be true for that to actually change the plan?"

## 3. Teamwork & collaboration (working in a team as a solo builder, giving/receiving help, cross-functional work, code review culture)

**Q1. You've built and run a fleet of ~80 sites and tools entirely on your own. When you join a team where you no longer own every decision, how do you adapt to working alongside other engineers?**
- *What they're really assessing:* Whether a strong solo builder can share ownership without becoming a bottleneck or a lone wolf.
- *Strong answer:* Building ~80 live sites solo taught me the difference between speed and correctness — solo I can move fast because the whole context lives in my head, but that same trait becomes a liability on a team if I don't externalize it. So my adaptation is deliberate: I write things down, I make my reasoning legible in PRs and design notes, and I optimize for the team's velocity over my own. Concretely, in my own fleet I've already started writing per-repo AGENTS.md files and architecture decision records even though I'm the only reader — precisely so the work isn't locked in my head. I actually enjoy the constraint of consensus because it catches blind spots I'd never see alone. And I'd rather be the person who unblocks three teammates than the one who ships the flashiest feature in isolation.
- *Watch out:* Implying that solo work is "better/faster" or that team process just slows you down.

**Q2. Tell me about a time you received tough or critical feedback on your work. What did you do with it?**
- *What they're really assessing:* Ego, coachability, ability to separate self from work.
- *Strong answer:* The clearest recent example is your own take-home — I built the cross-review PR bot, and the feedback I got back pushed on cases I'd under-handled, like how the bot behaves on large diffs and noisy false positives. My first reaction was mild defensiveness for about thirty seconds, then I realized the reviewer was right and I'd optimized for the happy path. I went back and specifically hardened the edge cases rather than arguing my original framing. What I've internalized from running everything solo is that I don't get feedback for free — nobody reviews my fleet but me — so when someone takes the time to critique my code, that's a gift I don't usually get. I try to respond to the strongest version of the critique, not the easiest one to dismiss.
- *Watch out:* Picking "feedback" that was actually a compliment, or subtly explaining why the feedback was wrong.

**Q3. As a solo builder, code review isn't part of your daily loop. What do you believe good code review culture looks like, and how would you contribute to it here?**
- *What they're really assessing:* Views on review as collaboration vs. gatekeeping — directly relevant to an AI code-review company.
- *Strong answer:* Good review culture is about raising the floor of the codebase without lowering anyone's morale — it's teaching, not gatekeeping. The best reviews I've seen separate blocking concerns (correctness, security) from preferences, and label them as such so the author knows what actually needs to change. Since I work solo, I've effectively used AI reviewers and my own delayed-second-pass as a substitute, which is exactly why Ostrya's cross-review product resonates with me — I've felt the gap it fills firsthand. As a reviewer I'd bias toward asking questions over issuing verdicts ("what happens if this input is empty?" beats "this is wrong"), and I'd always approve small, obviously-good changes fast so review doesn't become a bottleneck. And I'd take my own reviews the same way — assume the reviewer is trying to help the code, not judge me.
- *Watch out:* Treating review as pure gatekeeping, or dismissing it as unnecessary because "I test my own code."

**Q4. Describe a time you asked for help when you were stuck. How did you approach it?**
- *What they're really assessing:* Whether high-agency means "never asks for help" (a red flag) or knows when to pull others in.
- *Strong answer:* My instinct is to grind on a problem myself, and I've had to consciously fight that. A concrete case: I hit a wall on Android app signing and PWA packaging for getting my oriz PWAs into the Play Store — the headless Bubblewrap path kept failing and I burned real hours on it. Rather than keep silently bashing my head, I timeboxed it, documented exactly what I'd tried and the exact errors, and went to community threads and docs with a specific, reproducible question instead of a vague "it doesn't work." That got me to the PWABuilder cloud path quickly. The lesson I carry is that asking well — with your attempts and evidence attached — respects the other person's time and usually gets a better answer. On a team I'd set a timebox before asking, so I'm neither the person who blocks for a day nor the one who interrupts every five minutes.
- *Watch out:* Claiming you "never really get stuck" or that you always figure everything out alone.

**Q5. You'll work cross-functionally here — with a CEO, possibly designers, product, non-engineers. Tell me about a time you had to work with or communicate to a non-engineering stakeholder.**
- *What they're really assessing:* Can they translate technical work for non-technical audiences and collaborate across roles.
- *Strong answer:* In my day job I'm an SAP consultant at TCS, which is fundamentally a cross-functional translation role — I sit between business stakeholders who describe what they need in process terms and the technical system that has to deliver it. A recurring pattern is a stakeholder asking for something that's technically expensive but where a cheaper 80% solution meets the real underlying need, so my job is to surface that tradeoff in their language, not mine. I've learned to lead with the outcome and the cost, and only go into implementation detail if they want it. On my personal side, running a public-facing fleet also forces me to think about the end user who doesn't care about my architecture at all — they just want the tool to work. That user-first instinct is what I'd bring to working with product and design here.
- *Watch out:* Only describing engineer-to-engineer communication, or condescending framing of non-technical people.

**Q6. Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate or a decision. How did you handle it?**
- *What they're really assessing:* Can they disagree respectfully, commit after losing, and avoid ego-driven conflict.
- *Strong answer:* Honestly, most of my recent "disagreements" have been with myself across time — I'll make an architecture call, then a month later my own past decision fights my current needs, which is a good rehearsal for defending and then abandoning a position. A real example from consulting: I pushed for a cleaner long-term data approach while a lead wanted the faster tactical fix for a deadline. I made my case once, clearly, with the tradeoff spelled out — then when the call went the other way for legitimate timeline reasons, I committed fully and helped ship their version well rather than sulking or slow-walking it. Disagree-and-commit is real to me; the worst outcome is a passive-aggressive teammate who "told you so" later. I'd rather lose the argument and keep the trust.
- *Watch out:* A story where you "won" and were vindicated, or where you never truly let go of your position.

**Q7. Early-stage startups have thin teams — you'll wear many hats and occasionally cover for others. How do you feel about picking up work outside your comfort zone to help the team?**
- *What they're really assessing:* Startup grit, low ego about "not my job," genuine team-first attitude.
- *Strong answer:* This is honestly the mode I'm most comfortable in — running an ~80-property fleet solo means I'm already doing DevOps, front-end, data pipelines, AI integration, and Play Store release engineering, none of which is my "official" job title. I don't have a strong sense of "that's not my role"; I have a strong sense of "the thing needs to work." So covering for a teammate on something unfamiliar is just Tuesday for me — I'll ramp fast, ask focused questions, and hand it back in a state the owner can pick up cleanly. The one thing I'd guard against is quietly hoarding a domain because I picked it up in a pinch, so I'd document as I go and make sure the real owner stays the owner. At an early-stage company that breadth-plus-ship-it instinct is the whole point.
- *Watch out:* Signaling role rigidity, or over-promising that you can do everything without ramp-up.

**Q8. How do you make your work visible and keep teammates in the loop — especially given you're used to working with no one watching?**
- *What they're really assessing:* Proactive communication habits; whether a solo builder will "go dark."
- *Strong answer:* Working solo is actually where I learned this the hard way, because context I didn't write down would evaporate — so I've built a habit of externalizing state even when I'm the only audience: AGENTS.md files, decision records, and small persistent memory notes across sessions so future-me isn't lost. On a team that same habit translates directly into proactive updates — short written status, flagging blockers early rather than at the deadline, and making PRs self-explanatory so a reviewer doesn't have to reconstruct my thinking. My rule is to communicate progress before I'm asked, and to raise bad news fast, because surprises are what erode trust. I'd rather over-communicate slightly in the first weeks and dial it back than go dark and have people wonder where I am.
- *Watch out:* Assuming you'll just "post when there's something to show" or treating status updates as overhead.

**Q9. Tell me about a time you helped a teammate grow, unblocked someone, or shared knowledge. What did you do?**
- *What they're really assessing:* Generosity, mentorship instinct, whether they lift others vs. only optimize for self.
- *Strong answer:* A lot of my knowledge-sharing shows up as building things other people can reuse — for instance, I turned my hard-won learnings into shared, reusable setups like a common Android keystore path and packaging recipe, and canonical config blocks I sync across repos, so the next person (including future-me) doesn't re-solve the solved problem. In my JEE-prep days and since, I've been the person who explains the underlying "why" rather than just handing over the answer, because a solution you understand is one you can extend. On a team I'd default to pairing over just fixing something for someone, and to leaving the codebase a little more self-documenting than I found it. The most satisfying version of help is the kind that makes itself unnecessary next time.
- *Watch out:* No genuine example, or framing help as "I just did it for them" (creating dependency, not growth).

**Q10. This is your chance — what would you like to ask me about how the team works here?** *(a question to ask the CEO)*
- *Why it lands:* "As an early-stage AI dev-tooling company, how does the team actually use your own cross-review product internally — and where does it still fall short of what you wish it did?" This shows you've used the take-home product, you think in terms of dogfooding and honest product gaps, and you're already reasoning like an owner about the roadmap rather than just a hire.
- *Follow-up:* "When a teammate and the AI reviewer disagree on a PR, how do you want humans to weigh the machine's opinion — where's the line between trusting the tool and overriding it?"

- *Why it lands:* "How do you, as founders, personally use your own product internally on your own codebase — and what's the one thing it still can't do that you wish it could?" It signals I care about dogfooding and honest product truth, not a sales pitch, and it invites the CEO to talk about the real gap — which is where I'd want to contribute.
- *Follow-up:* "When the agent and a senior engineer disagree on a review, whose call is it today — and how do you want that to evolve as the product gets more trusted?"

## 10. Smart QUESTIONS TO ASK THE CEO back (vision, hardest problem, what success looks like at 6 months, culture, why they started Ostrya, what they look for) — for these, give the QUESTION + why it lands well

**Q1. What's the vision for Ostrya over the next few years — what does the company look like if everything goes right?**
- *Why it lands:* Signals you're evaluating the mission for the long haul, not just chasing a job; lets the CEO talk about what energizes them, which builds rapport.
- *Follow-up:* "Where does AI-assisted code review — the cross-review bot I built the take-home for — sit inside that bigger picture? Is it a wedge or the core product?"

**Q2. What's the single hardest problem you're wrestling with right now — technical or otherwise?**
- *Why it lands:* Shows you want to run toward the hard part, not the polished pitch; a high-agency builder question that tells the CEO you'd own real problems, not just tickets.
- *Follow-up:* "Is that a problem the next engineer could meaningfully move, or is it founder-level for now? I'd rather know where I can actually make a dent in the first quarter."

**Q3. What does success look like for this role at the six-month mark — how will you know the hire worked out?**
- *Why it lands:* Reveals whether expectations are concrete or fuzzy, and shows you think in outcomes and ownership rather than activity. It also lets you self-select honestly.
- *Follow-up:* "And what would a disappointing-but-not-failing six months look like? Knowing the failure mode helps me prioritize better than just knowing the win."

**Q4. How would you describe the culture in a way that's true even on a bad week — not the careers-page version?**
- *Why it lands:* Politely signals you can tell aspiration from reality and want the honest picture; culture-fit rounds reward candidates who probe for authenticity without cynicism.
- *Follow-up:* "What's a behavior that gets someone respect here, and one that quietly loses it? The unwritten rules tell me more than the values slide."

**Q5. Why did you start Ostrya — what was the moment or frustration that made this worth doing?**
- *Why it lands:* Founders light up on origin stories, and their answer reveals conviction depth and whether the mission is durable or opportunistic. It's the most genuine rapport-builder in the set.
- *Follow-up:* "Has that original thesis held up, or has the market taught you something that changed it? I'm curious how much the product today matches the day-one idea."

**Q6. When you've made a great hire in an early engineer, what did they have that the resume never showed?**
- *Why it lands:* Surfaces the CEO's real bar for culture-fit and lets you map your own strengths — solo end-to-end shipping, high agency — onto exactly what they value.
- *Follow-up:* "And the mis-hires — what was the common thread? I'd rather know the anti-pattern so I don't become it."

**Q7. How do decisions actually get made here right now — where's the line between 'just ship it' and 'check with someone first'?**
- *Why it lands:* Shows you care about moving fast responsibly and want to calibrate autonomy, which is exactly the instinct an early-stage CEO wants from someone who'll work unsupervised.
- *Follow-up:* "As you grow past the first handful of engineers, what part of that speed are you most afraid of losing?"

**Q8. Ostrya is building AI dev tooling in a space that's moving weekly — how do you decide what to build versus what to let the model providers eventually absorb?**
- *Why it lands:* Demonstrates you understand the existential strategy question for an AI-agents/dev-tooling startup and think like a builder about defensibility, not just a coder taking orders.
- *Follow-up:* "For something like the cross-review bot, is the moat the workflow and taste, the eval quality, or the integration surface? That'd shape how I'd build it."

**Q9. What do you want to be true about how this team works that isn't quite true yet — the culture you're still building toward?**
- *Why it lands:* Invites the CEO to be candid about gaps and shows you see joining early as helping shape culture, not just consuming it — a genuine ownership signal.
- *Follow-up:* "Is that something a new engineer can help set the tone on, or is it yours to drive? I've had to build my own norms running my projects solo, so I'm comfortable either way."

**Q10. What's the one thing you'd want the person in this seat to care about deeply — even obsess over — that you can't put in a job description?**
- *Why it lands:* Closes the loop on values alignment, gives the CEO the last word on what truly matters, and lets you honestly confirm whether that obsession matches yours (shipping, agent quality, user trust).
- *Follow-up:* "Does that match what pulled you into this problem, or is it a gap you're hiring to cover? Either answer tells me a lot about where I'd fit."
